ChanceConstraints.jl

A package for computing and optimizing with Joint Chance Constraints in Julia.

ChanceConstraints.jl allows to use joint chance constraints with Gaussian uncertainty with JuMP models. It computes the joint probability and its gradient directly, instead of relying on conservative approximations; and adds them as constraints to a predefined JuMP model.

Why chance constraints

Many optimization problems depend on quantities that are not known when the decision is made: inflows to a reservoir, demand, solar and wind generation, as well as grid availability. Two common ways of handling this both have drawbacks:

  • Deterministic models replace the uncertain quantity by its expected value. The resulting solution often fails when the realization differs from the mean.
  • Worst-case (robust) models protect against every realization. This is safe but often far too expensive.

A chance constraint sits in between. It asks that a constraint holds with at least a chosen probability $p$ (for example 90% or 95%), and accepts a small violation risk $1 - p$ in exchange for a cheaper solution. The risk level is an explicit modelling choice, and the cost of reliability can be adjusted by changing $p$.

Individual vs. joint chance constraints

Suppose a decision $x$ must satisfy $m$ uncertain constraints, for example one per time period.

An individual chance constraint treats each constraint separately:

\[\mathbb{P}\big(g_i(x,\xi) \le 0\big) \ge p, \qquad i = 1,\dots,m .\]

Each constraint is satisfied with probability $p$, but this says nothing about all of them holding at the same time.

A joint chance constraint requires all constraints to hold simultaneously:

\[\mathbb{P}\big(g_i(x,\xi) \le 0,\; i = 1,\dots,m\big) \ge p .\]

When the constraints are correlated, for instance because they depend on cumulative inflows or on the same weather conditions, individual constraints can be misleading: the probability that everything holds is generally lower than $p$, so the system is less reliable than the model suggests.

The possibly striking difference between individual and joint chance constraints has been widely studied (e.g., Van Ackooij et al., 2010, p. 547, Berthold et al., 2022, p. 34).

Bonferroni vs. exact computation

A classical way to avoid the nonlinearity and compelexity in computing the joint probability is the Bonferroni approximation (Nemirovski and Shapiro, 2006). Since

\[\mathbb{P}\Big(\bigcup_{i} \{g_i > 0\}\Big) \le \sum_{i=1}^m \mathbb{P}(g_i > 0),\]

requiring each individual constraint to hold with probability $1 - (1-p)/m$ guarantees the joint constraint at level $p$. This gives a model with only individual constraints, each with a closed form for Gaussian data.

The price is conservatism. The bound ignores correlation and assumes the violation events are disjoint, so the resulting solution is typically more expensive than necessary, and the gap grows with $m$.

This package instead evaluates the joint probability itself. For Gaussian uncertainty, it is a multivariate normal CDF, which can be computed accurately with the methods described below.

The math

Setting. Let $ξ \sim N(μ, Σ)$ be a Gaussian random vector. The constraints treated by this package have the form

\[\mathbb{P}\big(\xi \le x\big) \ge p , \]

where $x$ is a vector of JuMP variables or affine expressions of the decision variables.

Individual case. For a single component $ξ_i \sim N(μ_i, Σ_{ii})$, with $σ_i = √Σ_{ii}$ and $Φ$ the standard normal CDF,

\[\mathbb{P}(\xi_i \le x_i) \ge p \iff x_i \ge \mu_i + \sigma_i\,\Phi^{-1}(p) ,\]

a closed-form deterministic constraint.

Joint case. The probability

\[F(x) = \mathbb{P}(\xi \le x) = \Phi_\Sigma\big(x-\mu\big)\]

is a multivariate normal CDF. It has no closed form for $m > 2$, but it is smooth and log-concave in $x$, so the feasible set ${x : F(x) ≥ p}$ is convex.

Reliability windows. A constraint need not cover the whole horizon. The arguments passed to functions of this package define windows of $κ + 1$ consecutive periods and their starting points, each required to hold jointly with probability $p$.

Computation. Two approaches are available.

  • Quasi-Monte Carlo (QMC) (compute_with_QMC and add_JCC_QMC). The CDF value and its gradient are evaluated numerically with a low-discrepancy sequence, following the algorithm of Genz (1992) implemented in Julia using the library MvNormalCDF. The gradient is passed to the solver.
  • Spherical-radial decomposition (SRD) (compute_with_SRD andadd_JCC_SRD). Writing $ξ = μ + R·L·u$, with $Σ = LLᵀ$, $u$ uniform on the unit sphere and $R$ chi-distributed, the probability becomes an expectation over directions $u$ of a one-dimensional chi CDF, computed along the ray from the mean to the boundary. SRD requires the mean to be strictly feasible, $h(x) > μ$.

Useful starting points are the Julia documentation and Julia Data Science.

JuMP. JuMP is a modelling language for optimization. A model has variables, an objective, constraints and a solver:

using JuMP, Ipopt

model = Model(Ipopt.Optimizer)       # choose a solver
@variable(model, x[1:3] >= 0)        # decision variables
@constraint(model, sum(x) <= 10)     # constraints
@objective(model, Max, 2x[1] + x[2] + x[3])
optimize!(model)

objective_value(model)
value.(x)

Because the joint probability is a nonlinear function of $x$, this package needs a nonlinear solver such as Ipopt, which is the solver used in the examples.

See the JuMP tutorials for more.

A first simple model

Find the cheapest $x$ such that $P(ξ ≤ x) ≥ 0.9$:

using ChanceConstraints, Ipopt

μ = [5.0, 2.0, 4.0, 3.0]    # Mean
Σ = [ 2.0  0.5  0.3 -0.1;   # Covariance
      0.5  2.2  0.7  0.4;
      0.3  0.7  1.6 -0.2;
     -0.1  0.4 -0.2  1.8]
p = 0.9                     # Probability level
κ = 3                       # Window length (e.g., outage duration in hours)

model = Model(Ipopt.Optimizer)
@variable(model, x[1:4] >= 0)
@objective(model, Min, sum(x))
add_JCC_QMC(model, x, [1], κ, Σ, μ, p)
optimize!(model)

References

This package grew out of research on the economic dispatch of weakly connected mini-grids under uncertainty around solar and demand forecasts, as well as main grid outage events. The research is presented in

  • Ouanes, N., et al. (2025). Optimizing the economic dispatch of weakly-connected mini-grids under uncertainty using joint chance constraints. Annals of Operations Research, 344, 499–531 (2025). https://doi.org/10.1007/s10479-024-06287-9

The methods implemented in this package build on the following theoretical work:

  • Genz, A. (1992). Numerical computation of multivariate normal probabilities. Journal of Computational and Graphical Statistics, 1(2), 141-149. https://doi.org/10.2307/1390838
  • Van Ackooij, W., & Henrion, R. (2014). Gradient formulae for nonlinear probabilistic constraints with gaussian and gaussian-like distributions. SIAM Journal on Optimization, 24(4), 1864–1889. https://doi.org/10.1137/130922689
  • Prékopa, A. (1995). Stochastic programming. Dordrecht: Kluwer Academic Publishers.
  • Prékopa, A., & Sántai, T. (1978). Flood control reservoir system design using stochastic programming. In M. L. Balinski & C. Lemarechal (Eds.), Mathematical programming in use (pp. 138–151). Berlin: Springer.

The implementation relies on the following software:

  • MvNormalCDF.jl, a Julia implementation of the Genz algorithm for multivariate normal probabilities, used to evaluate the joint probability.
